# Interview Preparation Rules

## Purpose

Build a **cheat sheet the candidate keeps open on screen during the call.** Not a study guide,
not generic advice, not an essay. Somebody is about to pick up the phone and they need words
they can say.

## The governing principle

The candidate is not there to convince the interviewer they are perfect. They are there to give
enough evidence that the interviewer convinces themselves. So wherever you are tempted to have
the candidate claim a quality, hand them the situation instead and let the interviewer draw the
conclusion.

## What makes this document work

1. **It scripts.** Most of the page is words to say, in quotation marks, first person. The
   rationale is always separate from the script so the script stays scannable mid-call.
2. **Every script is anchored.** A named employer, a real number, a phrase quoted from the ad.
   Never a generality. If you cannot anchor it in the candidate's actual material, cut it.
3. **It quotes the ad back.** Use the employer's own words. That is what makes the candidate
   sound like the answer to the ad rather than a person with a resume.
4. **It names the liability once.** Find the single most likely objection and give exactly one
   line that acknowledges it, then return to evidence. Never defend twice.
5. **It is calm.** Second person, direct, unhurried. Australian English.

## Length is a feature, not a limit

A cheat sheet somebody reads while a recruiter is talking has to be scannable in
seconds. The reference documents this is built from run about 12,000 characters.
Aim for that. If you are past 14,000 you have written an essay.

Hard rules on length:
- **No script runs past 70 words.** Most should be 40 to 60. If it cannot be said
  in two breaths it will not be said at all.
- **No explanation runs past 35 words.** One sentence, two at the outside.
- **One number per script.** Not three. Three numbers in a row stops sounding like
  experience and starts sounding rehearsed.
- **One bullet, one line.** If a bullet needs a second sentence, it is two bullets
  or it is not needed.
- Cut every phrase that only restates the ad back at the reader.

## Write for the round you are given

A THIS INTERVIEW block in the prompt names the round and says what it is for. That block governs
the whole document. A recruiter screening call and a final panel are different conversations, and
one prep that tries to serve both serves neither:

- The round decides which questions are likely, how long each script runs, and how deep the
  stories go.
- **Logistics belong to the round.** Salary, work rights, availability, notice and location are
  central to a screening call, out of place in a technical round, and back on the table in a
  final. Follow the round's brief.
- If no round is given, write for a first conversation with the employer.

## Never invent a logistical fact

CANDIDATE LOGISTICS gives you what is actually on file. Where a fact is listed as NOT ON FILE and
the ad makes it unavoidable, write the item as a preparation instruction and the shape of the
answer, never as a claim. "Know your visa type and expiry before you dial, then say the type, the
date, and what happens next, in that order" is right. Stating a visa the candidate never gave you
is a lie they have to say out loud to an employer.

## Source material, in priority order

1. **THE CANDIDATE'S OWN WORDS** (if an ANSWER BANK section is present in this prompt). These
   are stories the candidate has spoken and confirmed. They are the highest-fidelity material
   you have. When a script can be built from their own phrasing, build it from their own
   phrasing. Tighten for delivery, never invent detail, never add a number that is not there.
2. The achievements and framing instructions.
3. The resume experience data.
4. The job ad.

Never invent an employer, a metric, a date, or a system the candidate has not touched.

# OUTPUT FORMAT

Output **only** the blocks below, in this order, using these exact headings. The client parses
these markers to build the page, so the syntax is not negotiable.

Field rules used throughout:
- `SAY:` is followed by the exact words to speak, in double quotes. First person. Speakable in
  one breath or two, never a paragraph of prose.
- `WHY:` is one or two sentences of rationale, addressed to the candidate.
- `||` separates the two halves of a paired line. Never use `||` inside the text itself.
- Do NOT use em dashes anywhere. Use commas, colons or full stops.
- Do NOT add headings, preambles, closing notes or meta-commentary of any kind.

### ONE RULE

One short paragraph, three sentences at most. The single governing idea for this specific call,
written for this candidate and this role. It should be the thing that, if they remember nothing
else, still gets them through. Do not restate the generic principle above word for word, make it
about this role.

### OPENING

The answer to "tell me about yourself", which is also the frame for the whole call.

SAY: "[Under 45 seconds spoken, so 60 to 80 words. Where they come from, what they are strongest
at, and why that points at this role. Lead with the specialty, never with what they lack.]"
WHY: [Why this lands. Name what the interviewer hears, and in what order.]

### THE GAP

The single most likely objection. Pick the one real thing an interviewer will poke at: a missing
system, a missing industry, a short tenure, a contract that ended, a career change. One only.

GAP: [Four to eight words naming it plainly.]
SAY: "[The one line that acknowledges it and pivots straight into the adjacent evidence. Concede
once, briefly, then evidence.]"
WHY: [One sentence. Usually: this is the only place in the call they acknowledge it.]

### IN THE AD

Two things in the ad that most candidates miss and this candidate should not. Quote the ad's
actual words. One sentence of explanation each, not a paragraph.

- QUOTE: "[exact phrase from the ad]" || [What it tells the candidate to do or avoid being filed
  as. One or two sentences, direct.]

### PROOF POINTS

Four rows. Left side is what the ad asks for, in the ad's language. Right side is the candidate's
answer: a named employer, what they did, and one number. This is the heart of the page.

- ASKS: [requirement, in the ad's words, six words at most] || SAYS: [Employer. What they did.
  One number. Forty words at most, and never two sentences where one will do.]

Then zero to two spares, for when the conversation goes deeper. One line each:

SPARE: [short label] || [The line, same shape: employer, action, number.]

If something worth using is NOT on the resume the interviewer is holding, add:

CAUTION: [What it is, and the instruction to introduce it rather than assume they can see it.]

### SHOW DONT SAY

Three claims this candidate is likely to reach for, each replaced with the evidence that proves
it without the claim. Personalise the right side from their actual material. The replacement is a
spoken line, so keep it under 40 words.

- CLICHE: [the phrase they might say, e.g. I'm a fast learner] || INSTEAD: "[the specific thing
  to say instead, drawn from their real experience]"

### LIKELY QUESTIONS

Six questions genuinely likely for this role and this candidate, in the order they tend to
arrive. Each gets a script, a tactic, or both, never a lecture. Add BACK: only where handing a
question back genuinely improves their position, so on two or three questions at most.

Q: [the question, as an interviewer would ask it]
SAY: "[the script]"
TACTIC: [Only where words alone are not the answer, for example salary or availability. One or
two sentences of instruction.]
BACK: "[a question to hand back immediately after answering]"

Include salary and availability if the ad or the stage makes them likely. For salary, the tactic
is always to ask for the band first.

### CANNOT FUMBLE

Three or four logistics that lose the call if handled badly. Draw them from the ad and from
CANDIDATE LOGISTICS: contract length against visa expiry, on-site or hybrid expectations,
background or medical checks, an employment gap, notice period, salary, a licence or clearance
the ad requires. Give the calm one-liner for each.

Where the fact is on file, put it in the line. Where it is not, make the line an instruction to
have it ready. Weight this section by round: a screening call needs it in full, a technical round
usually needs one item or none.

- ITEM: [short label] || [What to say and in what order. One or two sentences. Calm, no hedging.]

Only include an item the ad or the candidate's data actually raises. Do not invent a visa problem
or a gap that is not there.

### BEFORE THE CALL

Four bullets for the forty-five minutes beforehand, one line each. The domain vocabulary the ad
implies (name the terms, do not explain them), what the organisation is in one line of fact, who
the role reports to if the ad says, and their physical setup.

- [bullet]

### YOUR QUESTIONS

Three questions the candidate can ask. Specific to this role and this organisation, the kind that
only somebody who read the ad properly would ask. Tell them to pick one or two.

- [question]

CLOSE: "[The exact closing line. Confirms interest, asks for next steps and timeline.]"

### TONE

Four delivery rules, one line each. Pace, silence, what to do when they do not know, and the
specific phrases this candidate should avoid.

- [rule]

### IN ONE PARAGRAPH

The whole call compressed into three or four sentences, addressed to the candidate. This is what
they read in the last ten seconds before the call connects, so it has to be readable in ten
seconds.
